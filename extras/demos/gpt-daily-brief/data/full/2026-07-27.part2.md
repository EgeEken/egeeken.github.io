> “From the purely economic point of view, it is a pre-eminent means for the realisation of surplus value.”

— Rosa Luxemburg, *The Accumulation of Capital*, chapter 32, “Militarism as a Province of Accumulation.” [Primary text](https://www.marxists.org/archive/luxemburg/1913/accumulation-capital/ch32.htm)

Luxemburg’s argument is not simply that arms companies profit from war. Taxation concentrates purchasing power that would otherwise remain dispersed among workers and small producers, then converts it into regular state demand for a sector insulated from ordinary consumer choice. Militarism can therefore stabilize a field of accumulation while also supporting imperial expansion into non-capitalist territories.

The useful qualification is that military demand is not autonomously determined by “capital itself.” Budgets face fiscal limits, inter-capitalist conflict, military doctrine and state strategy. Her stronger insight survives: once a procurement ecosystem exists, factories, employment, regional dependence, lobbying and sunk research costs create a constituency for continued expenditure even when the original emergency changes.

## 8. Two philosophy and political fragments

### Hannah Arendt: violence may destroy power without creating it

> “Power and violence are opposites; where the one rules absolutely, the other is absent.”

— Hannah Arendt, *On Violence*. [Contemporary publication of the essay](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1969/02/27/a-special-supplement-reflections-on-violence/)

Arendt defines power as people acting in concert and violence as an instrument. A state may possess overwhelming weapons yet become politically brittle when commands are obeyed only through coercion; conversely, a movement may have power before it has control of the coercive apparatus. The distinction is analytically useful but not clean in practice: institutions usually combine authorization, administration and force. The question is which element reproduces obedience when routine legitimacy weakens.

### Ibn Khaldun: taxation as a symptom of dynastic decay

> “At the beginning of a dynasty, taxation yields a large revenue from small assessments.”

— Ibn Khaldun, *The Muqaddimah*, book III, chapter 36, Franz Rosenthal translation. [Passage and bibliographic context](https://newworldeconomics.com/ibn-khaldun-taxes-and-the-rise-and-decline-of-empire/)

Read in isolation, this resembles a medieval Laffer curve. In context, it is part of a theory of dynastic cycles: early rulers retain group solidarity, limited court expenditure and relatively restrained extraction; later courts expand luxury, bureaucracy and coercion, raise assessments, weaken production and still collect less. The claim is therefore political-sociological rather than a universal instruction to cut tax rates. The tax base erodes because the ruling order’s relation to producers has changed.

## 9. French usage: **avoir beau + infinitif**

**Avoir beau + infinitive** marks a concession, often an effort that fails to change the result: **“no matter how much…,” “even though…,”** or **“try as one might.”**

- *J’ai beau relire l’article, je ne vois toujours pas comment ils évitent la fuite de données.*
- *Il a beau être rapide, ce modèle consomme trop de mémoire pour l’embarqué.*
- *On aura beau ajouter des Canadair, sans prévention le risque continuera d’augmenter.*

The conjugated verb is **avoir**; the following verb stays in the infinitive. In speech, *j’ai beau essayer* can sound close to *j’ai beau essayé*, but the latter is ungrammatical. Compared with *bien que*, the construction often emphasizes persistence or ineffectiveness rather than merely stating a contrast. ## 11. Unrelated fact: Venice’s “wells” were mostly rainwater filtration cisterns

Historic Venice had almost no dependable fresh groundwater beneath its dense urban islands. Many of the stone wellheads visible in campi did not open onto aquifers: surrounding roofs and paved surfaces directed rainwater into underground basins, where layers of sand filtered it before it collected around a central shaft. A clay envelope isolated the basin from the lagoon’s brackish water.

This was simultaneously hydraulic engineering and social administration. Specialist *pozzeri* built the systems; parish officials held keys and opened local wells at fixed times, with water rationed during droughts. As the population grew, rainwater was insufficient, so *acquaroli* carried fresh water from the Brenta and replenished the cisterns. The system explains the old Venetian paradox recorded by Marin Sanudo: a city surrounded by water but without water fit to drink. ## 12. Puzzle: Kavka’s toxin

A billionaire offers you **$1 million** under these rules:

- At midnight tonight, a perfectly reliable scanner checks whether you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow at noon.
- The toxin will make you painfully ill for one day but cause no lasting damage.
- If the scanner finds the sincere intention, the money is transferred immediately and cannot be taken back.
- At noon tomorrow, drinking the toxin no longer affects whether you receive the money.

You strongly prefer **money and no illness** to **money and illness**, and either to **no money**.

Can a rational person sincerely form the intention tonight? Once noon arrives, drinking is pointless; but knowing that now seems to prevent the intention on which payment depends. Is the obstacle a defect in rationality, a fact about what intentions are, or merely the absence of a commitment device? [Gregory Kavka, “The Toxin Puzzle”](https://academic.oup.com/analysis/article/43/1/33/264435)
