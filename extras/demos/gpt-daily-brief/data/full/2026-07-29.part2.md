The line is often flattened into “the state is bad, therefore abolish administration.” Marx’s sharper claim concerns **institutional inheritance**. A new class or coalition that simply occupies an apparatus designed for command from above may reproduce its separation from society, even under a new programme. The difficult question is not only who controls the machine, but which capacities must be dismantled, which must be democratised, and how coordination can be preserved without restoring an autonomous bureaucracy over the producers.

## 8. Two philosophy and political fragments

### Blaise Pascal: justice needs force, but force can redefine justice

> “Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical.”

— Blaise Pascal, *Pensées*, fragment 298 in the W. F. Trotter translation. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/18269/pg18269-images.html)

Pascal is not offering a stable constitutional synthesis. The surrounding fragment is darker: justice is disputed while force is immediately recognisable, so societies are tempted to label whatever power can enforce as just. The useful distinction is between giving coercive capacity to a defensible rule and retroactively treating successful coercion as the rule’s justification.

### Friedrich Nietzsche: sincere certainty can be harder to correct than deception

> “Convictions are more dangerous enemies of truth than lies.”

— Friedrich Nietzsche, *Human, All Too Human*, aphorism 483, translated by Helen Zimmern. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/51935/pg51935-images.html)

A liar normally distinguishes the proposition from the truth being concealed; a conviction can absorb the believer’s identity and reorganise what counts as counter-evidence. Nietzsche’s point is not that commitment is always irrational, but that sincerity is not an epistemic guarantee. A political movement, scientific programme or moral cause may need stronger internal mechanisms for adversarial testing precisely when its adherents are most honest.

## 9. French usage: **tenir au courant**, **tenir au jus**

**Tenir quelqu’un au courant** is the neutral everyday way to say **“keep someone informed / keep someone posted.”**

- *Je te tiens au courant dès que j’ai la réponse.*
- *Tiens-moi au courant si l’horaire change.*
- *Vous pouvez me tenir au courant de l’avancement ?*

**Tenir au jus** means the same thing but is distinctly familiar:

- *Tiens-moi au jus pour ce soir.*
- *Je vous tiens au jus* is possible in a relaxed team, but can sound too casual with an unfamiliar senior person or in formal correspondence.

For a more formal register, use **tenir informé**: *Merci de me tenir informé de la suite.* The Académie française also recommends these established forms over the office Franglais *mettre quelqu’un dans la loop*. ## 11. Unrelated fact: Marshallese stick charts model the ocean rather than drawing a conventional coastline

Marshallese navigators built **stick charts** from palm ribs, fibre and sometimes shells. Shells could mark islands, while straight and curved sticks represented dominant swells and the reflection, refraction or disturbance of waves around low atolls. Some charts, such as *rebbelib*, represented relationships among particular islands; schematic *mattang* models taught general swell behaviour.

They were not simply portable maps read at sea. The models were used to train and organise the navigator’s memory, and were generally not carried on voyages. At sea, navigators interpreted how multiple swell systems pitched and rolled the canoe, combining that embodied signal with stars, winds, clouds and wildlife. The chart therefore encoded a physical model and a pedagogy: it taught which changes in wave structure reveal land that may still be below the horizon. ## 12. Puzzle: the St. Petersburg paradox

A fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads.

- Heads on toss 1 pays **$2**.
- First heads on toss 2 pays **$4**.
- First heads on toss 3 pays **$8**.
- In general, first heads on toss `n` pays **$2ⁿ**.

The expected monetary value is

`(1/2 × $2) + (1/4 × $4) + (1/8 × $8) + … = $1 + $1 + $1 + …`

so it is unbounded. A naive expected-value rule therefore says that **any finite entry fee** is worth paying. Yet paying your entire savings for one play seems plainly irrational, and there is a 75% chance you receive no more than $4.

What is the principal failure?

1. Money has diminishing marginal utility, so monetary expectation is the wrong objective.
2. A one-shot decision should not be priced by an ensemble average dominated by arbitrarily rare outcomes.
3. The game is incoherent because no finite bank can guarantee every possible payout.
4. Expected value remains correct; reluctance to pay a large fee is merely irrational risk aversion.
5. The original puzzle needs a different decision rule entirely.

How much would you actually pay, and which answer best explains that ceiling? [Background](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2022/entries/paradox-stpetersburg/)
