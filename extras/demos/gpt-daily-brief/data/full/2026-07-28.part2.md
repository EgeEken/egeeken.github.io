> “A house may be large or small; as long as the neighboring houses are likewise small, it satisfies all social requirement for a residence.”

— Karl Marx, *Wage Labour and Capital*, chapter 6. [Primary text](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1847/wage-labour/ch06.htm)

Marx’s point is not that material standards do not matter. It is that a wage and the goods it buys are assessed inside a changing social relation. A dwelling can remain physically unchanged while becoming socially inadequate because productive capacity, surrounding consumption and the customary requirements of participation have moved.

This is useful against both crude immiseration claims and crude “living standards rose, therefore exploitation disappeared” replies. Capitalism can raise absolute consumption while also enlarging relative dependence and redefining what is necessary to work, communicate, travel or belong. The relevant question is not only how many goods workers possess, but how the social product and decision-making power are divided as the benchmark of a normal life changes.

## 8. Two philosophy and political fragments

### Adam Ferguson: institutions can emerge without a designer

> “the result of human action, but not the execution of any human design”

— Adam Ferguson, *An Essay on the History of Civil Society*, part III, section II. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8646/pg8646.html)

Ferguson’s formulation is often used to defend spontaneous order against central planning. Its sharper implication is methodological: an institution’s function cannot be inferred from anyone’s intention, because repeated local adaptations can produce a structure no participant foresaw.

That does not make emergent orders benign. Segregation, monopoly conventions and administrative path dependence can also be undesigned. The productive friction is between constructivist confidence—someone can specify the whole system—and conservative naturalization—the system emerged, so it must be efficient or legitimate.

### Frederick Douglass: a demand matters when it changes the balance of power

> “Power concedes nothing without a demand. It never did and it never will.”

— Frederick Douglass, “West India Emancipation,” 1857. [Source and attribution](https://www.loc.gov/item/2023632631/)

Douglass rejects the idea that injustice disappears through the moral development of rulers alone. Reform becomes likely when organized people impose political, economic or reputational costs on refusal.

The sentence is sometimes reduced to a celebration of rhetorical militancy. A demand by itself is cheap. Its force comes from organization, persistence, coalition and the capacity to disrupt ordinary reproduction. Conversely, institutions can grant concessions pre-emptively precisely because they anticipate such pressure; the absence of an open confrontation does not prove that power acted voluntarily.

## 9. French usage: **à la rigueur**

**À la rigueur** means **“at a pinch,” “if absolutely necessary,”** or **“at the outer limit of what is acceptable.”** It usually introduces a reluctant fallback, not the preferred plan.

- *À la rigueur, je peux passer demain, mais pas avant 19 heures.*
- *Le modèle tient sur le M2 Pro ; à la rigueur, on peut le quantifier pour la 3070 Ti.*
- *On peut supprimer cette figure à la rigueur, mais pas le tableau des résultats.*

It overlaps with **à la limite**, but often sounds slightly more constrained or reluctant. It is not identical to **au pire**: *à la rigueur* names an acceptable last resort, whereas *au pire* introduces the worst-case contingency. Compare: *À la rigueur, on prend le métro ; au pire, on rentre à pied.* [Académie française](https://www.dictionnaire-academie.fr/article/A9R2651)

## 11. Unrelated fact: in Aymara time can place the known past in front and the unknown future behind

English and many European languages put the future “ahead” and the past “behind.” Research on Aymara, spoken across the Andes, documented a major contrasting pattern: words associated with **front/eye** also refer to the past, while **back/behind** terms refer to the future. Speakers’ spontaneous gestures reinforced the mapping—pastward references could be made in front of the body and future references behind it.

The proposed logic is epistemic rather than a reversed timeline. The past has been seen and is therefore in the field of vision; the future is unknown and cannot be seen, so it lies behind the speaker. The study does not show that every Aymara speaker always spatializes time this way, nor that grammar rigidly determines thought. It does show that the familiar “future ahead” metaphor is not cognitively mandatory, and that gesture can expose a conceptual mapping more clearly than translation alone. [Núñez and Sweetser, *Cognitive Science*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1207/s15516709cog0000_62)

## 12. Puzzle: the preface paradox

You finish a carefully researched book containing 300 factual claims.

- You have strong evidence for claim 1, so you rationally believe it.
- The same is true of claim 2, claim 3, and each individual claim through claim 300.
- You also know that books of this length almost always contain at least one error, and you have no special reason to think yours is flawless. You therefore rationally believe: **“At least one claim in this book is false.”**

Your beliefs now seem jointly inconsistent: you believe every claim, yet also believe their conjunction is false.

Which principle should give way?

1. Rational belief is not closed under conjunction.
2. At least one individually well-supported claim should be suspended, even when you cannot identify it.
3. The general belief in your own fallibility is not rational once every particular claim passes review.
4. The inconsistency is acceptable because rational belief need not form a logically consistent set.

What would make one resolution better than the others? [Background](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemic-paradoxes/)
